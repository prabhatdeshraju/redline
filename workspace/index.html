What this document commits you to

This agreement engages you as an independent contractor for a fixed fee, payable ninety days after the Client accepts the work. It assigns everything you produce — and the tools you produce it with — to the Client on creation. It requires you to cover the Client’s losses arising from the work without any ceiling, and it makes you personally liable for your company’s obligations under it.

What these clauses mean in law depends on your jurisdiction and on how the agreement is executed. Redline describes what the document says; it does not advise you whether to sign.

Schedule of flags

Ranked by what each clause permits at worst, never by how common it is.

  1. Matches one of your red lines: “I will not give a personal guarantee.”

    What it permits at worst

    Your own assets — not your company’s — can be used to satisfy a claim under this agreement. The guarantee outlives the agreement that created it.

    Whether a court would enforce it, and against which assets, depends on your jurisdiction and how the guarantee was executed.

    Counter-offer to send back

    Delete clause 12.4. If the Client needs security, offer instead: “The Contractor’s liability under this Agreement is limited to the fees paid or payable under it, and no personal guarantee is given.”

  2. What it permits at worst

    You pay the Client’s legal costs and losses for a third party’s claim even where you did nothing wrong, with no ceiling and no relation to what you were paid.

    Courts do read some indemnities more narrowly than they are written; how narrowly depends on the wording and the jurisdiction.

    Counter-offer to send back

    Tie it to fault and cap it: “The Contractor shall indemnify the Client against claims arising from the Contractor’s gross negligence or wilful misconduct, up to the total fees paid under this Agreement.”

  3. Matches one of your red lines: “I keep ownership of the tools and libraries I bring.”

    What it permits at worst

    The tooling you arrived with, and everything you build alongside the deliverable, transfers to the Client on creation. Reuse and portfolio rights go with it.

    Most commissioned freelance work does not legally qualify as work made for hire even where an agreement says it is; what the clause then achieves varies.

    Counter-offer to send back

    Separate the deliverable from your toolkit: “The Client owns the deliverables on payment in full. The Contractor retains ownership of pre-existing and general-purpose tools, libraries and know-how, and grants the Client a licence to use them as embedded in the deliverables.”

  4. Matches one of your red lines: “No payment terms longer than 30 days.” Your red lines are disqualifying whatever a clause’s rank — this clause sits at Medium severity and still fails your own test.

    What it permits at worst

    You carry ninety days of unpaid work, and the clock only starts when the Client chooses to accept. Exposure is bounded by the contract value, which is why it ranks at Medium.

    Statutory late-payment rules exist in some jurisdictions and may override parts of this; the document does not mention them.

    Counter-offer to send back

    Start the clock on invoice and shorten it: “Fees shall be invoiced on completion and payable within thirty (30) days of invoice. Acceptance shall be deemed given if the Client does not respond within ten (10) business days.”

Limitations of this inspection

  • No verdict. Redline never tells you whether to sign, and it is not legal advice.
  • Negotiable documents only. Terms of service and other take-it-or-leave-it documents are out of scope.
  • Typed text only. Scanned or photographed documents are not inspected.
  • No sentence, no flag. Where the exact wording cannot be quoted, the flag is withheld rather than approximated.

What Redline inspects

A negotiable document — one whose terms you still have standing to change. A client agreement, a statement of work, a vendor or service agreement, a lease.

What it will not inspect

  • Terms of service and other take-it-or-leave-it documents. There is nobody on the other side to accept a counter-offer.
  • Scanned or photographed documents. A misread character produces a quotation that is wrong in the one way you could not catch.
  • Documents you have already signed. Replacement wording is worth nothing once it is signed.

A terms-of-service upload is refused with this explanation today. What Redline should do with one instead is an open product decision.
